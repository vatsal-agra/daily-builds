THE LOOM OF SMALL THINGS
being nine fables of the village of Millbrook, written for the Loom project

I. THE WEAVER AND THE RIVER

In the village of Millbrook there lived a weaver named Etta who kept her loom
by the window so the morning light could fall across the threads. Every day
the river ran past her house, brown after rain and green after drought, and
every day Etta would call out to it as if it could hear her.

"Good morning, river," she said. "What did you carry today?"

The river never answered, but Etta imagined that it did. She imagined it
telling her about the mountain where it was born, about the mill wheel it
turned three towns over, about the fish that lived in its deepest pools and
the herons that stood patient on its banks. She wove these stories into her
cloth without meaning to, and so her blankets came out patterned like moving
water, blue and brown and green in long unbroken lines.

One autumn the river flooded. It rose past the millhouse, past the bridge,
past the low stone wall that marked the edge of the market square. The
villagers gathered sandbags and shouted instructions at one another, and in
the middle of the commotion Etta carried her loom to the highest room of her
house and kept weaving.

"You are still my river," she told it, "even when you are too big for your
banks."

When the water finally went down, it left a layer of black silt across every
garden in Millbrook, and the villagers complained for a week about the mess.
But in the spring the gardens grew taller and greener than anyone remembered,
fed by everything the river had carried down from the mountain. Etta wove a
new blanket that year with threads of black running through the blue, and
she called it "the river remembers," and it sold for more than any blanket
she had made before.

The moral the old women of Millbrook told their daughters was this: what
floods you today may feed you tomorrow, and the weaver who keeps working
through the flood is the one who understands the pattern.

II. THE CARPENTER WHO COUNTED NAILS

Down the lane from Etta's house lived a carpenter named Tobin, who was known
throughout Millbrook for counting everything. He counted the nails in his
apron before he began a job and counted them again when he finished, and if
even one nail was missing he would search the sawdust on his floor until he
found it.

"Why do you count so carefully?" his apprentice asked him one day. "A single
nail is worth almost nothing."

Tobin set down his hammer and looked at the boy for a long moment.

"A house is nothing but small things added together," he said. "A nail, a
board, a joint, a coat of paint. If I am careless with the smallest thing,
why would you trust me with the roof over your head?"

The apprentice thought this was strange advice, and for a while he ignored
it, hammering nails in loosely and leaving off a few boards where no one
would notice. But the houses he built on his own began to creak in the
wind, and one bad winter a shutter he had hung carelessly tore free and
broke a neighbor's window.

He came back to Tobin the following spring and asked to be taught again,
this time properly. Tobin handed him the apron of nails without a word and
watched him count them, twice, before he picked up the hammer.

Years later, when the apprentice had his own apprentices, he told them the
same thing Tobin had told him: a house is nothing but small things added
together, and the carpenter who respects the nail will be trusted with the
roof.

III. THE BAKER'S PATIENCE

Millbrook's baker was a broad, quiet woman named Src who rose before dawn
every day of her life to light the oven. Her bread was famous three villages
over, and travelers sometimes went out of their way to pass through Millbrook
just to buy a loaf still warm from her oven.

A young baker from the next town once came to watch her work, hoping to
learn her secret. He watched her mix flour and water and a pinch of the
starter she had kept alive for twenty years, and he watched her knead the
dough with slow, even strokes.

"Is that all?" he asked, disappointed. "Flour, water, salt, and time?"

"That is all," Src said. "But you asked the wrong question. You should have
asked how much time."

She let her dough rise for a full day before she shaped it, and let the
shaped loaves rise again through a cold night, and only then did she bake
them, low and slow, in an oven that had been warming since midnight. The
young baker, impatient, tried to shortcut the process when he returned home.
He raised the oven temperature and cut the rising time in half, and his
bread came out pale and dense and sold for half the price.

He returned to Millbrook a second time, humbler, and asked Src to teach him
properly. She agreed, on one condition: that he would not ask her to make
it faster.

"Bread does not care how busy you are," she told him. "It rises when it is
ready, and not one minute before."

IV. THE CHILD WHO ASKED WHY

There was a child in Millbrook, no older than seven, who asked why about
everything. Why is the sky blue. Why does the river run downhill and never
up. Why do the geese leave in autumn and come back in spring. Why does
Tobin count his nails and why does Src wait so long for her bread to rise.

Most of the villagers found the questions tiring, but the old miller,
a man named Corwin who had lived through three wars and buried two wives,
never once told the child to be quiet.

"Why does the mill wheel turn?" the child asked him one afternoon, sitting
on an overturned bucket by the millpond.

"Because the water pushes it," Corwin said.

"Why does the water push it?"

"Because water always moves toward the lowest place it can find, and my
wheel happens to be in its way."

"Why does water do that?"

Corwin laughed, a long rolling laugh that startled the pigeons from the
mill roof. "I do not know," he admitted. "Some things simply are, and a
wise miller learns to work with them instead of asking why forever."

The child was quiet for a moment, turning this over.

"But you just told me why the wheel turns," the child said. "You only
stopped asking why when you ran out of answers."

Corwin looked at the child for a long moment, and then he laughed again,
even louder than before, and told the child to come back the next day so
they could ask more questions together. The two of them spent the rest of
that autumn walking the riverbank, asking why about everything they saw,
and neither of them ran out of questions before the first snow.

V. THE TWO GARDENERS

There were two gardeners in Millbrook who shared a low stone wall between
their plots. One gardener, a man named Farro, planted only what he knew
would sell well at the market: cabbages, onions, and a reliable row of
potatoes. The other gardener, a woman named Iris, planted whatever seed
caught her fancy, including flowers that had no market value at all.

"You waste half your garden on things no one will buy," Farro told her one
spring, not unkindly.

"You waste half your garden on things no one will remember," Iris answered,
and went back to planting her foxgloves.

That summer a blight came through Millbrook and struck every field of
cabbage and onion in the valley. Farro's harvest failed almost entirely,
and he spent a hard winter trading favors to keep his family fed. Iris's
flowers, untouched by the blight, drew bees from three villages over, and
those bees pollinated every fruit tree in Millbrook that year, filling the
orchards with more apples and pears than anyone could remember.

The following spring Farro planted half his plot in flowers, not because
he had stopped needing cabbages, but because he had learned that a garden
which only produces what can be counted is a garden with no room left for
what cannot.

VI. THE LOOM ITSELF

It is worth saying something about Etta's loom, since it gives this
collection its name. It was an old loom, built by her grandmother's
grandmother, and it had outlived four owners and one fire that burned half
the house around it. The wood was dark with age and oil, and the treadles
had been worn smooth by generations of feet.

Etta once asked her mother why they had never replaced it with something
newer.

"A new loom knows nothing," her mother said. "This loom has made ten
thousand blankets. It knows exactly how much tension a thread can bear
before it snaps, and exactly how a shuttle should fly across the warp
to land where it belongs. A new loom would have to learn all of that
again, one broken thread at a time."

"But it is only wood and string," Etta said.

"So is a house," her mother answered. "So is a bridge. So, if you think
about it long enough, is almost everything that lasts."

Etta kept the loom her whole life, and passed it to her own daughter, who
added a small brass plate to the frame with nine words engraved on it: what
is patched and mended still holds the pattern.

VII. THE MESSENGER WHO COUNTED MILES

A young messenger named Dell was sent from Millbrook to the coast with a
letter so urgent that he was told to run the whole way without stopping. He
set off at a sprint, certain that speed alone would win him the errand, and
by midday he had exhausted himself on the first steep hill outside the
village and could go no further.

An old shepherd found him resting by the roadside and asked where he was
headed.

"To the coast," Dell said, "with an urgent letter, but I cannot run another
step."

"Then walk," the shepherd said. "A letter that arrives tomorrow is worth
more than a letter that never arrives at all."

Dell walked the rest of the way, pacing himself against the miles instead
of against the clock, and though he arrived a full day later than he had
hoped, he arrived. The letter he carried, it turned out, prevented a
dispute between two fishing families that might otherwise have lasted a
generation. No one asked whether he had run or walked. They only asked
whether the letter had come, and it had.

From then on Dell carried a small stone in his pocket on every errand, one
stone for each mile he judged himself capable of running before he needed
to slow to a walk, and he was never again found exhausted by the roadside.

VIII. THE ARGUMENT AT THE MILL

One winter the villagers of Millbrook argued for three days about whether
the mill should be rebuilt with a second wheel, doubling its output but
doubling its cost as well. Farro wanted the second wheel, certain it would
pay for itself within two harvests. Iris was against it, certain the river
could not reliably turn two wheels through a dry summer. Tobin was asked to
build it and Src was asked to help pay for it, and Corwin, the old miller,
was asked to settle the argument since it was, after all, his mill.

Corwin listened to every argument twice before he spoke.

"I have turned one wheel for forty years," he said at last, "and I have
seen six summers so dry the river could barely turn that one. A second
wheel is a fine idea in a year of plenty and a foolish one in a year of
drought, and I cannot tell you in advance which kind of year is coming."

"Then what should we do?" Farro asked.

"Build the second wheel," Corwin said, "but build it so it can be taken
down again in a single afternoon, and store the parts dry, and use it only
in the years the river can bear it."

Tobin built exactly that, a wheel that could be raised out of the water on
a simple pulley system whenever the millpond ran low, and for the rest of
Corwin's life the village never again argued about whether the second wheel
had been a mistake, because it was never a mistake for longer than one dry
season at a time.

IX. WHAT THE VILLAGE REMEMBERED

Long after Etta and Tobin and Src and Corwin had all passed from Millbrook,
the village still told their stories, though the details drifted a little
with each telling, the way a river drifts a little from its old banks
after every flood. Children were told to count their nails like Tobin, to
be patient like Src, to keep asking why like the child on the bucket, to
save a little garden for what could not be sold like Iris, and to pace
themselves like Dell on the long road to the coast.

No single fable explained the village of Millbrook. It took all nine,
woven together the way Etta wove her threads, each one carrying a little
of the pattern that no single thread could carry alone. And that, the old
women said, was the whole of the lesson: a village, like a blanket, like a
loom, like a river, is nothing but small things added together, faithfully
repeated, until a pattern appears that no one thread could have shown you
by itself.
