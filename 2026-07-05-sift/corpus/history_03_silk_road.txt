The Silk Road as a Network, Not a Road

The Silk Road was never a single route but a shifting web of overland and maritime paths connecting China, Central Asia, the Indian subcontinent, the Middle East, and Europe. Silk traveled west while glass, wool textiles, and horses moved east, but goods were only part of what spread along these routes: Buddhism reached China this way, papermaking technology reached the Islamic world and then Europe, and the Black Death likely traveled west along the same caravan routes in the fourteenth century. Oasis cities like Samarkand and Kashgar grew wealthy as waypoints where caravans rested and traded, and the Mongol Empire's control over much of the route in the thirteenth century briefly made overland travel safer and taxation more predictable for merchants.
