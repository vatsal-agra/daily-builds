Quantum Mechanics and the Limits of Certainty

Quantum mechanics describes particles not as having a single definite position and momentum at once, but as existing in a superposition described by a wave function until a measurement forces a definite outcome, a transition often called wave function collapse. Heisenberg's uncertainty principle formalizes this: the more precisely a particle's position is known, the less precisely its momentum can be known simultaneously, a limit built into the physics itself rather than a limitation of measuring instruments. Quantum entanglement links two particles so that measuring one instantly determines information about the other regardless of the distance separating them, a correlation so strong that Einstein initially doubted it, though experiments have since confirmed it repeatedly. These same strange properties, superposition and entanglement, are exactly what quantum computers attempt to exploit to perform certain calculations far faster than any classical computer could.
