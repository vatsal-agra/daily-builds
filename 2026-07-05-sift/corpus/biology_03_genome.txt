What the Human Genome Project Actually Found

The Human Genome Project, completed in 2003, sequenced the roughly three billion base pairs of human DNA, but one of its biggest surprises was how few genes it found: around twenty thousand, far fewer than early estimates that assumed a straightforward gene-per-trait relationship. Much of the genome does not code for protein at all, and research since the project has shown that large portions of this noncoding DNA regulate when and where genes are switched on, rather than being simple junk. A single gene can produce multiple different proteins through alternative splicing, which helps explain how a relatively modest gene count can support the complexity of a human body. The project's reference genome also made modern genetic medicine possible, since comparing a patient's DNA against a shared reference is how most genetic tests identify meaningful variants.
