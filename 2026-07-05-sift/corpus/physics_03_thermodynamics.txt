The Laws of Thermodynamics in Plain Terms

The first law of thermodynamics is simply energy conservation: energy can change form, from chemical to thermal to mechanical, but the total amount in a closed system never increases or decreases. The second law introduces entropy, a measure of disorder that tends to increase over time in any closed system, which is why heat flows from a hot object to a cooler one and never spontaneously the other way around. This directional arrow of entropy is one of the few laws of physics that distinguishes past from future at all, since most other fundamental equations work identically whether time runs forward or backward. The third law states that a system's entropy approaches a minimum, fixed value as its temperature approaches absolute zero, which is also why reaching absolute zero exactly is considered physically unattainable, only ever approachable in the laboratory.
