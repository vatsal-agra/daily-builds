THE LAMPWRIGHT'S APPRENTICE

Part One: The Order of Small Lights

Before the Hollow Vale had a Warden or a bridge worth naming, it had lamps, and the lamps had a keeper, and the keeper had an apprentice named Tomas who was, by his own frequent admission, better at breaking lamps than lighting them.

"You have broken four lamps this week," said Master Priya, holding up the shards of the fifth so Tomas could not pretend otherwise. "Four. There are only forty lamps in the whole valley. You have broken one tenth of them."

"The glass was already cracked," Tomas said.

"The glass was already cracked because you dropped it last week and I have not yet had time to replace it. That is not the glass's fault. That is entirely your fault, twice."

Tomas had no answer for that, so he swept up the shards instead, which was at least a task he could not fail at, provided he was careful with his fingers.

Master Priya was the last of the Order of Small Lights, a guild that had once kept lamps burning along every road in the valley so that no traveler need walk in true darkness, not even on the moonless nights when the old stories said the Grey Tide first tested the edges of the world. Most people no longer believed the old stories. Priya kept the lamps burning anyway, on the theory that a story did not need to be believed to be worth preparing for.

Part Two: The Lamp That Would Not Light

The trouble began with the lamp at the eastern crossroads, the farthest one from the tower, which Tomas was finally trusted to tend alone. He filled the oil, trimmed the wick, and struck the flint exactly as he had been taught a hundred times -- and the lamp would not light.

He tried again. Sparks caught the wick and died instantly, as if something invisible pinched them out between his fingers and the glass.

He walked the two hours back to the tower in the dark, which he was not supposed to do, and told Master Priya exactly what had happened, which he very much did not want to do.

She did not scold him. That worried him more than any scolding would have.

"Show me," she said, and took her own case of flints down from the shelf without being asked twice.

Part Three: What Waited at the Crossroads

The crossroads lamp stood exactly as Tomas had left it, glass clean, wick trimmed, oil full. Master Priya knelt before it the way she might kneel before an altar, and struck her flint once.

The spark caught, held for the length of a breath, and went out -- not blown out, not smothered, but pinched, exactly as Tomas had described, as though the dark itself had reached up and closed two fingers around the flame.

"How long has this crossroads been dark?" Priya asked, very quietly, not turning around.

"Since last night. Maybe longer. I don't know when anyone last checked it."

"Then it has had a full night and most of a day to grow comfortable here." She rose, brushing dust from her knees, and looked east down the crossroad, toward the low hills that marked the edge of the valley proper. "Tomas. I need you to remember something, and I need you to remember it exactly, because I am not going to have time to explain it twice. The dark does not go away because you refuse to be afraid of it. The dark goes away because a light insists on being there anyway, over and over, in the same place, until the dark gets tired of losing."

"That doesn't sound like a plan," Tomas said. "That sounds like stubbornness."

"They are the same thing," Priya said, "practiced for long enough."

Part Four: Forty Lamps

She sent Tomas running that same night to every lamp still standing in the valley -- there were, despite his four broken ones, thirty-five left -- with one instruction: light every single one, and stay with it until it either held or failed, and if it failed, light it again, and stay again, for as long as it took.

Tomas did not sleep that night. He went from lamp to lamp with flint and oil, and at each one the flame caught easily, ordinary and warm, exactly as it should. Only at the eastern crossroads did the dark push back, and only there did Priya stay the whole night through, striking her flint again and again, patient as stone, while the pinched-out sparks grew fewer and further between as the hours wore on.

By the time Tomas returned near dawn, having lit and relit every other lamp in the valley twice over, he found Priya sitting cross-legged before the crossroads lamp with a small, steady flame finally burning inside the glass, her flint case empty beside her and her hands trembling with exhaustion rather than cold.

"It held," she said, before he could ask. "Everything holds, eventually, if enough small lights refuse to give up their place at the same time. That is the whole of the Order's secret, Tomas, and now you know it, and you did not even have to break a lamp to learn it."

"I broke four lamps this week."

"You did," Priya agreed, and for the first time in longer than Tomas could remember, she smiled at him without a single trace of exasperation in it. "Try not to make it five before spring."

Part Five: The Order Continues

Tomas never did become as careful with glass as Master Priya would have liked, and two more lamps did not survive his apprenticeship, bringing his lifetime total to a number he preferred not to discuss. But he learned to walk the crossroads at dusk without hurrying, flint case in hand, and in later years, long after Priya had passed the Order fully into his keeping, travelers on the eastern road would say that no matter how far they had come or how late the hour, there was always, somehow, a small warm light waiting at the crossroads to meet them -- and that the keeper who tended it never once, in all the years anyone could remember, let it go out.
