THE LOOM-WEAVER'S DAUGHTER
An original fable, written for this project.

CHAPTER ONE: THE QUIET VILLAGE

In a quiet village at the edge of a gray forest there lived a weaver named
Old Maren. Old Maren kept a wooden loom in the front room of her house, and
every morning before the sun had fully climbed the hills, she sat at the
loom and began to work. The threads went over and under, over and under,
and the shuttle clicked back and forth like a small patient heart.

Old Maren had a daughter named Tamsin. Tamsin had quick fingers and a
quicker mind, and she loved to watch the threads cross each other on the
loom. "Mother," said Tamsin, "why do you weave every single day?"

"Because a village needs cloth," said Old Maren, "and cloth does not weave
itself. Every thread must cross another thread, or the cloth falls apart."

Tamsin nodded, though she did not yet understand. She was young, and the
lesson of the loom would take many years to learn. Still, she sat beside
her mother every morning, and every morning she watched the threads cross,
over and under, over and under, until the pattern began to make sense to
her eyes if not yet to her heart.

CHAPTER TWO: THE TRAVELING MERCHANT

One autumn day a traveling merchant came to the quiet village. He wore a
long gray coat and carried a heavy pack, and he called out to every house
he passed, "Fine cloth for sale, fine cloth for sale, cloth from the far
cities, softer than any cloth you have seen!"

Old Maren stepped outside her house and looked at the merchant's cloth. She
ran her fingers over it slowly, the way she ran her fingers over her own
work each evening. "This cloth is fine," she said, "but it will not last
the winter. The threads are loose, and loose threads always come undone."

The merchant laughed. "What do you know of cloth, old woman?"

"I know that a village needs cloth that lasts," said Old Maren, "not cloth
that only looks fine in the autumn sun." She turned back to her loom, and
Tamsin, who had been listening at the window, felt a small fire of pride
light up inside her chest.

CHAPTER THREE: THE FIRST LESSON

That evening, Old Maren sat Tamsin down beside the loom and placed a small
shuttle in her hands. "It is time," said Old Maren, "for your first
lesson."

"What must I learn first?" asked Tamsin.

"You must learn that every thread has two directions," said Old Maren.
"The warp threads run the length of the cloth, from the top of the loom to
the bottom. The weft threads run across, from your left hand to your right
hand and back again. Over one warp thread, under the next, over the next,
under the next. This is the whole secret of the loom."

Tamsin took the shuttle and tried. Over one warp thread, under the next.
Her first row was crooked, and her second row was worse. "I will never
learn this," said Tamsin.

"Everyone says that on the first day," said Old Maren, smiling. "And every
weaver I have ever known has learned it by the hundredth day. Come back
tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that."

CHAPTER FOUR: THE HUNDRED DAYS

So Tamsin came back the next day, and the day after, and the day after
that. Over one warp thread, under the next. Her rows grew straighter. Her
hands grew steadier. She began to feel the rhythm of the loom the way she
felt her own heartbeat, without needing to think about each single beat.

On the thirtieth day, Old Maren said, "Your rows are straighter than mine
were at thirty days."

On the sixtieth day, Old Maren said, "You are ready to choose your own
pattern."

On the hundredth day, Tamsin wove a small piece of cloth entirely on her
own, from the first thread to the last, and when she held it up to the
window, the pattern caught the light like a piece of the gray forest turned
to gold.

"This is good cloth," said Old Maren, and for the first time she did not
add any correction after the praise. "This is cloth that will last the
winter."

CHAPTER FIVE: THE HARD WINTER

That winter was the hardest the village had known in many years. Snow fell
for three weeks without stopping, and the gray forest turned white and
silent. Many families ran short of warm cloth, for the traveling merchant's
loose-threaded goods had come undone exactly as Old Maren had warned, and
there was little time left to weave new cloth before the cold grew worse.

Old Maren and Tamsin worked together at the loom from morning until night.
Over one warp thread, under the next. Old Maren's hands were slower now
than they once were, but Tamsin's hands had grown fast and sure, and
between the two of them they wove cloth for every family in the village
before the worst of the winter arrived.

"We could not have done this alone," said Old Maren one night, when the
wind was loud against the shutters. "Your speed and my steadiness, woven
together, made enough cloth for everyone."

"Like warp and weft," said Tamsin.

"Exactly like warp and weft," said Old Maren.

CHAPTER SIX: THE MERCHANT RETURNS

In the spring, the traveling merchant came back through the quiet village,
his gray coat thinner than before and his pack much lighter. He stopped at
Old Maren's house, though this time he did not call out about fine cloth
from the far cities.

"I heard," said the merchant, "that this village survived the winter on
cloth woven by an old woman and a young girl."

"We survived the winter," said Old Maren, "on cloth woven by two weavers,
one old and one young, neither more important than the other."

The merchant looked at Tamsin, who was sitting at the loom, her shuttle
moving over one warp thread and under the next, steady as breathing. "Will
you sell me a length of that cloth?" he asked. "I will pay a fair price
this time, and I will tell every village between here and the far cities
where to find good, honest cloth."

"You may buy it," said Tamsin, speaking for the first time, "but only if
you promise to sell it honestly, and never call loose thread fine cloth
again."

The merchant considered this for a long moment, and then he laughed, not
unkindly. "That is a fair price indeed," he said. "I promise."

CHAPTER SEVEN: THE SECOND LESSON

Years passed, as years do, over one warp thread and under the next, and
Tamsin grew from a girl into a woman, and her hands grew as steady as her
mother's had once been. One morning Old Maren, whose hair had gone as gray
as the forest at the edge of the village, sat down beside Tamsin at the
loom and said, "It is time for your second lesson."

"I thought the first lesson was the whole secret of the loom," said
Tamsin.

"The first lesson teaches you to weave cloth," said Old Maren. "The second
lesson teaches you to weave a village. Listen carefully, for I will only
say this once. A thread alone is nothing. It breaks if you pull it, it
tangles if you drop it, it is lost if the wind takes it. But a thread
crossed with another thread becomes cloth, and cloth crossed with care
becomes something that can carry a family through a hard winter."

Tamsin looked at her mother's hands, folded now in her lap instead of
moving at the loom. "And people?" she asked. "Are people threads too?"

"People are the finest threads of all," said Old Maren, "for they can
choose, each morning, whether to cross another thread or to stand alone.
The quiet village was never quiet because nothing happened here. It was
quiet because every thread chose, morning after morning, to cross the
threads around it, over and under, over and under, until the crossing
itself became a kind of peace."

CHAPTER EIGHT: THE NEW WEAVER

When Old Maren grew too old to sit long at the loom, Tamsin took her place
each morning before the sun had fully climbed the hills, and the shuttle
clicked back and forth like a small patient heart, exactly as it had when
Tamsin was a girl. Children from the village came to watch the threads
cross on the loom, the way Tamsin herself had once watched, and one small
girl with quick fingers and a quicker mind asked, "Why do you weave every
single day?"

"Because a village needs cloth," said Tamsin, "and cloth does not weave
itself. Every thread must cross another thread, or the cloth falls apart."

The small girl nodded, though she did not yet understand. She was young,
and the lesson of the loom would take many years to learn. Still, she sat
beside Tamsin every morning, and every morning she watched the threads
cross, over and under, over and under, until the pattern began to make
sense to her eyes, if not yet to her heart.

And so the quiet village went on, morning after morning, thread after
thread, one weaver teaching the next, over one warp thread, under the
next, for as long as anyone in the gray forest could remember, and longer
still.

THE END
